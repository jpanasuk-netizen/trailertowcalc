Does My Trailer Need Brakes?

The brake decision starts from loaded weight, then from how the brakes are controlled.

Quick answer: For a trailer with a loaded weight of more than 1,500 lb, many states require a separate braking system and a breakaway switch (NHTSA towing safety). Electronically controlled brakes need an in-cab controller and wiring. Surge brakes are an independent hydraulic system at the hitch and may not be legal in your jurisdiction, so check your state's motor vehicle administration. Cross the safety chains under the tongue.

How to size it

Weigh the trailer the way you tow it. NHTSA says to use fully loaded weight, not dry weight, and a public scale is the way to get that number (NHTSA towing safety). If the loaded result is more than 1,500 lb, you are in the group many states cover with a separate braking system and a breakaway switch. Some states go further and require brakes on all axles. This page does not list those state codes. The motor vehicle administration in the state where you register and where you tow is the office that publishes them.

Pick the brake type only after that legal check. Electronically controlled brakes are not self-contained: they need a controller in the cab and wiring between the truck and the trailer. The controller is what lets you set braking from the driver's seat and what feeds the breakaway circuit's partner, the trailer brakes. Surge brakes do not use that controller. They are an independent hydraulic system at the hitch that applies when the trailer surges forward. They may not be legal where you tow. In either case, never connect the tow vehicle's hydraulic system to the trailer's.

The breakaway switch is the second half of the sentence in the NHTSA guide, not an optional add-on on a trailer that already needs brakes (NHTSA towing safety). It applies the trailer brakes if the coupler lets go. It is not a reason to skip chains. Safety chains are required by most states. Leave enough slack to turn, keep them off the pavement, and cross them under the tongue so the tongue is caught if the coupler fails.

Trailer breakaway kits

Lights are federal, separate from the state brake rule. The trailer needs taillights, brake lights, side marker lights, turn signals, and side and rear reflectors (NHTSA towing safety). The connector is a 4-way, or a 7-way when you also need a brake signal. A trailer over 8 ft wide may need permits. None of those items changes the hitch's weight rating. No hitch increases the maximum weight a vehicle can tow, and capacity is still limited to the lowest-rated component (CURT hitch classes). Look up GVWR, maximum trailer weight, maximum tongue weight, GCWR, and GAWR in the owner's manual, and match the hitch to those specifications (NHTSA towing safety).

Trailer safety chains

If you are deciding the brake question for a specific loaded weight, the hitch-class pages start at 2,000 lb, which is already over 1,500 lb, and go up through 15,000 lb. FAQ

At what weight do many states require trailer brakes?

When loaded trailer weight is more than 1,500 lb, many states require a separate braking system and a breakaway switch (https://dmna.ny.gov/nynm/training/NHTSA_TowingSafety.pdf). Some states require brakes on all axles. Use loaded weight, not dry weight, and weigh the trailer at a public scale.

What is the difference between electric and surge brakes?

Electronically controlled brakes need an in-cab controller and wiring. Surge brakes are an independent hydraulic system at the hitch (https://dmna.ny.gov/nynm/training/NHTSA_TowingSafety.pdf). Never connect the tow vehicle's hydraulic system to the trailer. Surge brakes may not be legal in some jurisdictions.

What does a breakaway switch do?

It is part of the requirement many states pair with brakes on a trailer loaded over 1,500 lb (https://dmna.ny.gov/nynm/training/NHTSA_TowingSafety.pdf). If the trailer separates, the switch applies the trailer brakes. Check it as part of the brake system, not as a substitute for safety chains.

How should the safety chains be hooked?

Most states require safety chains. Leave some slack for turns, do not let them drag, and cross them under the tongue (https://dmna.ny.gov/nynm/training/NHTSA_TowingSafety.pdf). Federal law also requires taillights, brake lights, side marker lights, turn signals, and side and rear reflectors, on a 4-way connector or a 7-way connector with a brake signal.
