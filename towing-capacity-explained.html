Towing capacity explained: tow rating vs GCWR vs payload

Short answer: your real towing capacity is the smallest of four ratings after your actual load is applied. Run the towing capacity check with your door-sticker numbers to see which one is binding.

Quick answer: Tow rating, GCWR, and payload must all pass at once — and on crew-cab half-tons, payload (including tongue weight) almost always runs out before the tow rating does. Run the calculator below to find your binding limit.

The four ratings

RatingWhat it capsWhere to find it
Tow ratingMaximum trailer weight the drivetrain is rated to pull (with WDH on most numbers you see)Owner's manual / towing guide for YOUR configuration
GCWRLoaded truck + loaded trailer, combinedDoor-jamb sticker / towing guide
Payload ratingEverything on the truck: passengers, cargo, tongue weight, hitch hardwareDoor-jamb sticker: GVWR − curb weight
Hitch classWeight the receiver itself can carry (Class III: 5,000 lb WDH / 500 lb tongue; Class IV: 10,000 / 1,000)Stamped on the hitch

The worked example that surprises everyone

A half-ton crew cab: 13,000 lb tow rating, 1,650 lb payload. You hook up a 7,000 lb camper packed for a two-week trip.

Tongue weight: 7,000 × 13% ≈ 900 lb. Add the WDH shank and head (~90 lb), four people (~420 lb), a cooler and bed gear (~200 lb): 1,610 lb of the 1,650 lb payload is gone. Fifty pounds of margin. The tow rating is barely half used — payload is the binding limit, and any option add-on weight the brochure didn't count puts you over.

That is why this site's first calculator computes every margin and shows you the lowest one. Never trust a single number.

GCWR is the silent one

Payload fits, tow rating fits — and GCWR still fails when the truck itself is heavy. Curb weight as optioned (4×4, diesel, sunroof, crew cab) plus passengers plus the trailer must stay under GCWR. Diesel half-tons are notorious: 1,000 lb heavier than the gas truck, same GVWR, meaning less payload and less GCWR margin for the same sticker tow rating.

What going over actually does

Over payload: rear suspension sits on the bump stops, front axle unloads (longer stops, vague steering, headlights in oncoming traffic), and tires run past their load range. Over GCWR: brakes work outside their designed thermal budget — fade is cumulative on mountain grades. This is not a ticket problem first; it is a crash mechanism. Check your rig with the capacity check before the first trip.
