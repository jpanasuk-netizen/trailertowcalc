What size hitch do I need for my trailer?

Hitch class is sized to your loaded trailer weight — and the class chart is only the first of three numbers to check.

Quick answer: Size the hitch class to your fully loaded trailer weight. CURT lists Class 1 up to 2,000 lb, Class 2 up to 3,500 lb, Class 3 up to 8,000 lb weight-carrying (12,000 lb with weight distribution), and Class 4 up to 10,000 lb. Never use the dry brochure weight.

Step 1: know the real weight

Size the hitch to loaded trailer weight, not the empty brochure weight. NHTSA's towing brochure says to use the fully loaded weight. Water, propane, and cargo belong in that total after you weigh them. This page does not assign a weight to a gallon of water or to a propane cylinder.

A scale ticket is the loaded weight. How to weigh a truck and trailer is the CAT Scale layout. This page does not add a percent to dry weight, and it does not state a scale price.

Step 4: weight distribution — the line most people miss

CURT rates weight distribution separately from weight-carrying. On Class 3 and Class 4 that column is up to 12,000 lb gross and 1,200 lb of tongue, above the weight-carrying caps. Use it when the rear squats, the trailer sways, or the headlights pitch up — the front axle has unloaded. A WDH redistributes tongue weight across the tow vehicle axles. It does not raise the vehicle's tow rating. Size the spring bars to the measured tongue weight, not to the biggest bars on the shelf. How to measure the tongue number first: the tongue weight guide.

The hitch is never the only limit

A Class 4 receiver, which CURT rates up to 10,000 lb weight-carrying, does not raise what the truck can tow. Four ratings all bind: the manufacturer's tow rating, the GCWR, the payload (tongue weight plus passengers plus cargo), and the hitch class. Read the truck's door-jamb sticker and owner's manual, and run all four in the towing calculator before buying hardware. Reading the rating stickers you already own

Every piece of this system is stamped, and the stamps beat the marketing: the receiver has a weight-carrying and weight-distributing rating on a sticker or stamp on its face; the ball mount has its capacity cast into the shank; the ball lists a gross-weight rating. CURT's ball chart runs 2,000 to 3,500 lb for 1-7/8 in, 3,500 to 12,000 lb for 2 in, and 6,000 to 30,000 lb for 2-5/16 in, and a given part can sit anywhere in that span. The coupler on the trailer lists the ball diameter it demands. Chain them: coupler → ball → mount → receiver, each rated at or above your loaded weight — and the weakest link, not the strongest, is the system's rating.

Two details people miss on used rigs: the receiver may have been on a previous vehicle with different ratings, and hitch bolts loosen. Use the torque in the hitch maker's instructions, and recheck the bolts after a sway. If the receiver has shifted on the frame, fix that before you tow.

Safety chains are part of the hitch too: cross them under the coupler so a coupler failure lets the tongue ride on the crossed chains instead of plowing the road, keep enough slack to turn but never enough to drag, and use rated S-hooks or shackles — never the spring clips that came with a bike rack.

Drop and rise: the height the class chart does not set

Class picks the rating. It does not pick how high the ball sits. CURT's adjustable-hitch guide measures both vehicles parked and level. Measure from the ground to the top inside edge of the receiver. In the guide's example that height is 21-3/4 inches. Level the trailer, then measure from the ground to the bottom edge of the coupler. In the same example that height is 15-1/2 inches. Subtract the coupler height from the receiver height. The example's difference is 6-1/4 inches of drop, which the guide describes as roughly 6 inches. CURT says to choose an adjustable hitch with at least 1 inch of extra rise or drop beyond the number you calculated. The guide says CURT adjustable hitches can raise the ball as much as 9 inches or drop it as much as 12 inches. An unlevel trailer, the same guide says, can increase sway and lengthen braking distance. The photographs and approximations in that guide are for demonstration and are not the specification of a particular hitch. The part you buy has its own range.

The mount's height does not replace its weight rating. On a 5,000 lb loaded trailer at 10 percent — the floor of CURT's 10–15 percent tongue band — TTC.tongueWeight returns 500 lb, inside the band, with targets of 500, 600, and 750 lb. CURT Class 3 weight-carrying covers up to 8,000 lb gross and 800 lb of tongue, so 500 lb of tongue fits that column. A 6-1/4 inch drop on a mount rated under that tongue still fails. Measure your own receiver and coupler; do not copy the 21-3/4 and 15-1/2 inch example onto a different truck. The connection order after the mount is chosen is how to hook up a trailer.

FAQ

What size hitch for a travel trailer?

Many travel trailers land in Class 3 or Class 4. CURT lists Class 3 to 8,000 lb weight-carrying and 12,000 lb with weight distribution, and travel-trailer balls as 2 in or 2-5/16 in. Match the coupler and the loaded weight, then run tongue weight through the calculator before you buy hardware.

Can my hitch be bigger than I need?

Up to Class III/IV on a vehicle that can handle it, yes — headroom is free capability. But the receiver's rating never raises the truck's: tow rating, GCWR, and payload still bind, and a Class V receiver bolted to a crossover is a Class V number with Class II reality.

Does tongue weight count against payload?

Yes. Tongue weight counts against payload. Hitch hardware counts too, once you have weighed it and entered it. This page does not publish a hitch weight. The payload check is in the calculator.
